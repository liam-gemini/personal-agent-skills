# How to Get Useful Answers to Your Questions — Knowledge Notes

## Source

- Author: Julia Evans
- Published: 2021-10-21
- Original: [How to get useful answers to your questions](https://jvns.ca/blog/2021/10/21/how-to-get-useful-answers-to-your-questions/)
- Form: Structured summary and operational interpretation; not a reproduction of the article.

## Central idea

Getting a useful answer is an active conversation skill. The questioner can narrow, redirect, clarify, and pause instead of passively accepting an explanation that sounds knowledgeable but does not resolve the original unknown.

## Common failure modes

- The answer repeats background the questioner already knows.
- The answer introduces information that may be true but does not advance the goal.
- The answer uses unfamiliar terminology, so relevant content remains inaccessible.
- The response ends without addressing the original question.
- Surprising information invalidates the questioner's model faster than they can update it.

## Core techniques

### Ask bounded or yes/no questions

A concrete hypothesis gives the answerer a narrow target and reduces irrelevant tangents. The useful answer is often more than yes or no, but the bounded form keeps elaboration attached to the user's actual uncertainty.

### State and update the current model

Explain the model being tested. When an answer contradicts it, pause to incorporate the new fact and identify its consequences before asking the next question.

### Interrupt constructively

Redirect a long tangent by acknowledging any useful context and asking a more specific question. Continuing an irrelevant explanation wastes time for both participants; polite interruption is cooperative, not hostile.

### Refuse unresolved non-answers

State what the response established, identify the remaining unknown, and ask again in a narrower form. Do not equate response length or confidence with resolution.

### Clarify terminology at the point of use

Ask what an unfamiliar term means in this context and how it differs from nearby concepts. Deferring terminology questions makes every later statement harder to evaluate.

### Make room to think

In real-time conversation, explicitly pause after surprising information. A short thinking break prevents a sequence of questions based on an obsolete model.

### Preserve confidence and alignment

Confusion may reflect a mismatched explanation rather than inability. Most answerers want to help but may not know the questioner's background. Both sides benefit from periodic alignment checks.

## Recovery checklist

1. What was the original unknown?
2. Which supported facts did the response establish?
3. What exact gap remains?
4. Is the problem scope, terminology, mechanism, premise, or evidence?
5. What single bounded follow-up is most likely to close that gap?
6. Should the conversation pause so the mental model can be updated?
7. If the answerer does not know, what newly established fact or next source still moves the inquiry forward?

## Boundary

Do not turn redirection into a debate over conversational performance. Preserve useful content, redirect toward the goal, and accept an explicit limit of knowledge when reached.
